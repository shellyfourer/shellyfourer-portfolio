> **TL;DR** — I decompiled the Uber rider app (v4.653.10004). Before Uber shows you a price, it assembles a **dossier** about you: who you are (including your rating and trip history), where you're going, how you pay, which pricing experiments you've been sorted into, and a **56-field fingerprint of your phone** (battery, VPN, GPS, even how you tap). What comes back isn't a single number — it's a **range** (`minFare`, `maxFare`, `pointEstimateFare`), and Uber shows you one point inside it. Your fare comes back tagged with an experiment **treatment group**. Your fare and your driver's pay are calculated by **separate algorithms**. I could see all of that in the code. The one thing I _couldn't_ see — the exact formula that picks your number — runs on Uber's servers, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

## Why I did this

Uber is worth around **$200 billion**. Last year riders paid it about **$193 billion** in gross bookings, and it kept roughly **$52 billion** in revenue. ([Uber FY2025 results](https://investor.uber.com/news-events/news/press-release-details/2026/Uber-Announces-Results-for-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-2025/default.aspx))

On a $100 ride, your driver takes home around $70, and Uber keeps the rest — before tips. I wanted to know _how_ a company builds the number you see, and whether there's anything in the app that explains why the same route never seems to cost the same twice.

So I pulled apart the actual app.

## How I did it (methodology)

Everything here is from **lawful static analysis of the shipped app** — no bypassing auth, no touching Uber's servers, no private data.

1. **Got the APK.** The Uber rider app (`v4.653.10004`, stable) from APKMirror — the same binary any Android user installs.
2. **Decompiled it with [jadx](https://github.com/skylot/jadx) 1.5.6.** The app is ~205 MB of code across 23 DEX files — about 173,000 Java classes once decompiled.
3. **Analyzed the pricing module** (`com.ubercab.presidio.pricing.core`) and the request/response models, plus string analysis across all the bytecode.
4. **Cross-checked the web app** with network capture, and the financials against Uber's SEC filings and an academic study.

Every class and field name below is real and quoted from the decompiled source.

## Finding 1 — Before you see a price, Uber assembles a dossier about you

When you ask Uber for a price, the app sends a request to its pricing service (`SurgeRequest`, and the fare request `RidersFareEstimateRequest`). That request is **not** just "where am I, where am I going." It bundles who you are, how you pay, which experiments you've been sorted into, and a 56-field fingerprint of your phone. Here is the actual payload, grouped — every field name quoted from the decompiled source, with the class it comes from:

```
WHO YOU ARE
  rating                            ← your rider rating        (Rider)
  displayRating                                                (Rider)
  totalCompletedRidersTripsCount    ← how many trips you've taken (Rider)
  riderUuid                         ← your account             (SurgeRequest)
  profileUUID / profileType         ← personal or business     (RidersFareEstimateRequest)
  email, mobileDigits                                          (Rider)

WHERE YOU'RE GOING
  deviceLocation                    ← where you are now        (SurgeRequest)
  pinLocation / destinationLocation ← pickup + drop-off        (SurgeRequest)
  viaLocations                      ← stops along the way      (RidersFareEstimateRequest)
  cityId                                                       (SurgeRequest)

HOW YOU PAY
  paymentProfileUUID                ← which payment method     (SurgeRequest)
  lastSelectedPaymentProfileUUID    ← your last-used card      (Rider)
  uberCashToggleOn, voucherUUID                                (RidersFareEstimateRequest)

WHICH EXPERIMENTS YOU'RE IN
  userExperiments                   ← your experiment buckets  (RidersFareEstimateRequest)
  experimentalFeaturesSpecification ← fare experiments         (RidersFareEstimateRequest)

YOUR DEVICE FINGERPRINT
  deviceData { ...56 fields... }    ← battery, VPN, GPS, taps  (DeviceData)
```

So "your phone sends 56 things" undersells it — **the 56 is just the device fingerprint.** Your rating, your trip count, your payment method, and your experiment assignment ride along in the _same_ request. (Your rating lives in a separate `Rider` object, not in `DeviceData` — but it's in the dossier all the same.)

**So what's the real total?** There isn't one honest single number — the request is _nested_ (objects inside objects, some of them lists), so any "Uber collects exactly N things" claim is fake precision. What I _can_ give you is the exact field count of each object on the price path, straight from the source — count them yourself:

| Object                      | Fields | What it is                                                           |
| --------------------------- | ------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `SurgeRequest`              | **31** | the pricing/surge request                                            |
| `RidersFareEstimateRequest` | **48** | the fare-estimate request                                            |
| `DeviceData`                | **56** | the device fingerprint (nested inside the request)                   |
| `Rider`                     | **37** | your rider profile — rating, trip count, payment, credits, referrals |

That's **four objects, 170+ top-level fields**, before you expand the nested ones (every `Location` adds lat/lng/accuracy/bearing; `impressions` is a list; `userExperiments` is a map). The honest statement isn't "Uber collects 172 things about you" — it's **"the price request is built from objects totaling well over a hundred fields, and the device fingerprint alone is 56."** Everything below is the 56, in full.

The fingerprinting is real, but it lives in a package literally named _fraud_, and nothing in the client shows it feeding the price. I'll come back to this.

## Finding 2 — Your fare isn't a number. It's a range.

This is the one that surprised me. The object Uber's server sends back for a fare estimate looks like this:

```java
// com.uber.model.core.generated.rtapi.models.auditablecontent.AuditableFareEstimateRange
public class AuditableFareEstimateRange {
    private final double minFare;            // the lowest your fare could be
    private final double maxFare;            // the highest your fare could be
    private final Double pointEstimateFare;  // the ONE number they show you
}
```

Your fare has a **minimum**, a **maximum**, and a **point estimate** — the single number that actually appears on your screen. You never see the range. You see the point. This model is referenced across 11 classes in the app (`fareRangeMin`, `fareRangeMax`, `pointEstimateString`, `updatedFareRange`), so it's not dead code — it's how fares are represented.

What I **can** say: the price you see was selected from a band Uber's server already computed.
What I **cannot** say: how the point inside that band is chosen. That math is server-side.

## Finding 3 — Fares run through an A/B-testing system

Your fare request carries an `ExperimentalFeaturesSpecification`, and the bytecode is full of fare-specific experiment keys:

```
fares_exp_point_fare_estimate
order_selection_m2_fares_treatment
u4b_xp_profiles_fare_binding
u4b_xp_fares_cache_invalidation
DEMAND_SHAPING_FARE_EXPLAINER
```

`xp` is Uber's prefix for experiments. In plain terms: **your fare runs through an experiment system**, the same way a store A/B tests a button color.

But it goes further than "presentation." The _response_ to your price request (`RidersFareEstimateResponse`) hands the client back your experiment assignment, tied to your fare:

```java
// RidersFareEstimateResponse
private final Map<String, FaresExperimentData> userExperiments;

// FaresExperimentData
public class FaresExperimentData {
    private final String treatmentGroup;              // which arm of the experiment you got
    private final Map<String, String> additionalData;
}

// ExperimentationMetadata — the server ranks multiple fare presentations and picks one
public class ExperimentationMetadata {
    private final FareContent styledPrimaryFare;              // the one you see
    private final ImmutableList<FareContent> rankedSecondaryFare; // the others it considered
}
```

Let me label this carefully, because this is exactly where honest teardowns and clickbait part ways:

- **Observed:** Uber sorts you into fare **treatment groups** and returns them _with_ your price. There is even an experiment named `u4b_xp_profiles_fare_binding` that ties your profile to your fare. The server computes **multiple** fare presentations and chooses a primary.
- **Inference (reasonable):** Because fares are tied to treatment groups, **which bucket you land in can change what you pay.** That is what A/B testing a price _means_.
- **Unknown:** Whether your bucket is assigned **randomly** (classic A/B) or **targeted using your dossier** (rating, history, device). That assignment logic runs on Uber's servers. It is not in the app.

Here's the part that makes the trail's end provable rather than a shrug: **I checked, and the client never computes a fare.** Nowhere in Uber's pricing code (`com.ubercab.presidio.pricing.core`) is there a formula, a multiplier, or a `fare = f(your data)` branch. The `FareBinder` classes do one thing — bind the server's number to a `TextView` for display (`void a(TextView)`). The app _reads_ `minFare`/`maxFare`/`pointEstimateFare` and shows you the point. The math left the phone on purpose. That's not an accident of architecture; it's the reason nobody — including me — can show you the formula from a decompile.

## Finding 4 — Your price and your driver's pay are two separate algorithms

Since Uber's 2023 pricing change, the amount you pay and the amount your driver earns are **computed independently**. A [2025 academic study](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.15278v1) tracked 114 London drivers before and after and found:

- Uber's median take rate rose from **25% to 29%**, with some trips exceeding **50%**.
- Uber's surplus per driver-hour rose **38%**.
- On higher-value fares, drivers earned _less_ per minute.

So you can pay more on the exact trip your driver earns less on — and Uber keeps the gap. On some rides, Uber now takes more than the driver does.

## Finding 5 — Your quoted price is signed and expires

```java
// BackwardCompatibleUpfrontFareSignatureData
public class UpfrontFareSignatureData {
    private final String signature;   // cryptographic signature of your fare
    private final Double issuedAt;    // when the price was issued
    private final Double expiresAt;   // when the price expires
}
```

Once your number is chosen, Uber **cryptographically signs it** and stamps an **expiry**. Your price locks to you for a few minutes, then refreshes. That's not a glitch — it's the design. It's also why your estimate changes if you sit on the screen too long.

## Finding 6 — Uber also built a "wait and save" nudge

The flip side of surge. Uber's app has a demand-shaping feature:

```java
// com.uber.model.core.generated.rtapi.services.marketplacerider.DemandShapingStatus
public class DemandShapingStatus {
    private final int    timeToRequestSec;   // how long to wait
    private final String savingsValue;       // how much you'd save by waiting
    private final String counterFooter;      // the countdown
}
```

Surge says "pay more now." Demand shaping says "wait N seconds, save $X." Both are Uber steering the marketplace to balance its own supply and demand — using your price as the lever.

## What I could NOT prove (and why that matters)

This is the part most "Uber exposed" videos skip, and skipping it is how they get things wrong.

- **The fare formula is server-side.** The app receives a range and a point. It does not compute them. I cannot show you the equation that picks your number, because it isn't in the app.
- **Battery / device data → price is unproven.** Yes, your battery level is in the `DeviceData` sent with ride requests. But that object is built by the fraud module, and no client code links it to pricing. "It's collected" is true. "It sets your price" is not something I can show.
- **A/B tested presentation ≠ proven price discrimination.** The experiment system is real, and your fare comes back tagged with a `treatmentGroup` (see Finding 3). But whether your bucket is assigned randomly or _targeted by your dossier_ is not visible from the client.
- **The experiment-targeting is the real unknown.** We can see that you're sorted into fare experiments, and that buckets carry fares. We cannot see what decides your bucket. If it's your rating, history, or device — that call happens server-side.

If someone tells you they "proved Uber charges you more because of your phone," ask them to show the server code. They can't — nobody outside Uber can. I confirmed _why_: the client has no fare math in it at all (Finding 3).

**How I'd actually close this gap (the honest next step):** not by guessing — by catching a real fare response at runtime. Run the app instrumented, request a ride, and read the actual `treatmentGroup` and `minFare`/`maxFare`/`pointEstimateFare` values the server sends back. That would turn "the field exists" into "here is my real bucket and my real hidden range." It's a bigger project (and Uber's own fraud checks — the `rooted`/`emulator` fields above — are built to make it hard), so it's the planned v2 of this teardown, not a claim I'm making today.

## But here's what _is_ proven: Uber price-discriminates by destination

I can't prove the server charges _you_ more for _your_ battery. But economists have proven something close, with real data and real peer review — and it's worth knowing the difference.

In **"Does Uber Benefit Travelers by Price Discrimination?"** (Chang, Winston & Yan, _Journal of Law and Economics_), researchers — one from the **Brookings Institution** — pulled real UberX fares for trips from three airports (**LAX, JFK, SFO**) to hotels, and tested whether Uber charges more when you're heading somewhere that signals you'll pay more.

The clever part is how they ruled out "it's just a longer trip." They matched **pairs of hotels within 0.1 miles of each other** — same stretch of road, same distance, same duration — and compared fares. They controlled for distance, trip duration, and time-of-day/day-of-week with fixed effects.

The result: fares are **positively and statistically significantly** tied to the destination hotel's room rate. A rider going to an above-median-priced hotel paid, on average, **$1.03 more in LA, $0.85 in NY, $0.63 in SF** — _on effectively the same trip._ In their words, Uber "exploits market segmentation with considerable precision."

That is **third-degree price discrimination** — pricing by _where you're going_ (a proxy for willingness-to-pay), proven on real trips. What no study has proven is **first-degree, personal-signal** discrimination: Uber charging _you_ more because of _your_ battery, phone, or history. The destination effect is documented. The "it reads your phone and overcharges you" claim is not.

This is the honest version of the story, and it's a stronger one: Uber demonstrably prices by _context_, and a $200B pricing operation almost certainly isn't leaving the 56 personal signals on the table — but "almost certainly" is not proof, and I'm not going to dress it up as one.

## So what is Uber, really?

Based on what's actually in the app: Uber isn't just a ride company. It's a **pricing machine** that happens to move you from A to B. It reads 56 signals about you, models your fare as a hidden range, A/B tests the presentation, signs and time-boxes the number, and pays your driver on a separate formula. $52 billion a year, one quietly-chosen number at a time.

## For builders: the monetization patterns here

If you're building anything that charges money, these are real, legitimate patterns worth understanding (and the reason this teardown is more than outrage bait):

- **Range pricing** — model a price as a band, not a point, and choose where to land.
- **Price experimentation** — A/B test what converts, carefully and legally.
- **Decoupling the two sides of a marketplace** — what the buyer pays and what the supplier earns don't have to be the same formula.
- **Time-boxed, signed quotes** — lock a price to a session so it can't be tampered with or stale.

## Questions people asked

**Isn't a price range normal? Every estimate has a min and max.**
A displayed _estimate range_ to the user is normal. What's notable here is that the app receives a `minFare`, a `maxFare`, **and** a separate `pointEstimateFare` — a single chosen point — and that point is what you're shown and charged against. You see the point, not the band it came from. That's the part worth knowing.

**Does my battery level actually change my price?**
I can't show that it does, and I won't claim it. `batteryLevel` is a real field in the `DeviceData` sent with ride requests — but that object is assembled by Uber's fraud module (`com.ubercab.fraud`), and no code in the app links it to the fare. "It's collected" is true. "It sets your price" is not something the app proves.

**Do the 56 things Uber collects about me actually change my price?**
Not that anyone can prove. Here's the honest split. **Proven:** Uber charges different fares based on your _destination_ — a peer-reviewed study ([Chang, Winston & Yan](https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/jle/) in the _Journal of Law and Economics_) found riders heading to pricier hotels paid up to ~$1 more on effectively the same trip. **Not proven:** that any of the 56 _device_ signals (battery, VPN, phone model, how you tap) feed your price. The famous "low battery = higher price" claim traces back to a 2016 interview where Uber's own research head said low battery is a strong _predictor of willingness to pay_ — but Uber says it doesn't use it, and no rigorous study has shown it does (one small newspaper test found a 6% difference; that's an anecdote, not proof). Even the FTC's 2025 "surveillance pricing" study documented personal-data pricing across industries but didn't pin a specific device signal to a specific Uber fare. So: destination-based discrimination is real and documented; personal-signal discrimination is plausible but unproven.

**Why can't you just show the formula that picks my number?**
Because it isn't in the app. The client sends your request and receives a range with a chosen point. The logic that computes those numbers runs on Uber's servers, which I did not access and can't see. Anyone showing you "Uber's pricing formula" from a decompile is showing you the _client's_ handling of the result, not the formula.

**Is this the same on iPhone?**
This teardown is the Android app (`v4.653.10004`). The iOS app is a separate binary I didn't analyze here. The server behavior is the same account either way, but I only claim what I verified on Android.

**Does using a VPN change my price?**
`connectedVpn` is one of the fields sent in `DeviceData` — so Uber knows whether you're on a VPN. Whether it affects price, same answer as battery: not shown in the client, not something I'll claim.

**Is any of this illegal?**
Nothing here shows illegal behavior. Dynamic pricing, device-based fraud detection, and A/B testing are all legal and common. The point of the teardown isn't "gotcha, it's illegal" — it's "here is how the machine is actually built."

**Why would Uber run pricing on servers instead of the app?**
Control and secrecy. Server-side pricing can be changed instantly for everyone, can't be inspected by people like me, and keeps the actual model private. The client only ever gets the result.
